Patients Who Could See but Couldn’t Recognize Faces

Case File 001 • Prosopagnosia • Student-led medical mystery

When vision works—but recognition doesn’t. A look at the striking condition where faces are seen, yet remain unfamiliar.

In 1947, German neurologist Joachim Bodamer was baffled by injured soldiers he had treated a few years earlier. These soldiers could see, but struggled to identify faces. Although similar cases had been noted throughout history, Bodamer’s detailed observations left a lasting mark. He introduced the term in his German paper Die Prosop-Agnosie.

The term prosopagnosia comes from classical Greek roots and translates to “agnosia for faces.”

Bodamer’s Observations

Patient S

Patient S was a 24-year-old soldier who suffered the most from this condition. He was originally completely blind after his brain injury, then eventually regained sight—though with complications. Patient S was color-blind, alexic (acquired dyslexia or “word blindness”), and had several other visual difficulties.

  • Patient S knew what a face was, but could not recognize whose face it was—not even genders.
  • He distinguished men and women using hair, hats, and other external features.
  • He relied on hats, glasses, uniforms, and similar cues to “learn” people’s faces.
  • He could not even recognize his own face in a mirror.
  • He could not reliably distinguish human faces from animal faces.

How the Brain Decodes a Face

Our eyes capture images visually:

  • Light enters through the pupil and lens, reaching the retina.
  • Cones and rods in the retina convert light into nerve signals.
  • These signals travel via the optic nerve to the brain.

According to Zhen et al. (2013), the most frequently localized regions for face processing are in the occipitotemporal cortex, including:

  • Fusiform gyrus (FG) / fusiform face area (FFA)
  • Inferior occipital gyrus (IOG) / occipital face area (OFA)
  • Posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS)

Roles:

  • FG and IOG: Analyze stationary, physical features (eye shape, nose structure).
  • pSTS: Processes dynamic features (eye gaze, expressions, lip movements) to support social interaction.

Other regions, such as the amygdala and insula, also contribute to emotional and contextual aspects of face perception.

What Exactly Is Prosopagnosia?

Prosopagnosia is a neurological disorder characterized by the inability to recognize faces.

There are two main forms:

Acquired Prosopagnosia

Results from brain injury. Clinicians now recognize that it can be triggered by:

  • Stroke
  • Trauma
  • Encephalitis
  • Surgical resection of the temporal lobe

Developmental (Inherited) Prosopagnosia

Likely has a genetic component. Multiple family members across generations can be affected. Patients with developmental/juvenile prosopagnosia struggle with face recognition throughout their lives.

When Faces Lose Their Meaning

Prosopagnosia shows us that seeing is not the same as knowing. The eyes may detect a face, but the brain gives it meaning, identity, and familiarity. When that connection is lost, even the people we love can become strangers.
